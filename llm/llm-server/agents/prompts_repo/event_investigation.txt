**Evidence Discipline (MANDATORY):**
1.  **Stay inside the incident window.** The Incident Window above is when this event was actually firing. Scope every log, metric, and trace query to that window (with a few minutes of margin), and prefer the evidence already collected on the event — it was captured in-window. Do NOT fetch broad relative ranges like "last 2h" or "last 24h" anchored to now: you are investigating after the fact, and such queries return activity from after the incident.
2.  **Post-window evidence cannot be the root cause.** An error or failure first observed after the incident window ended cannot explain this occurrence. Treat it as context for a possible recurrence, never as the cause.
3.  **Already-resolved alerts are weak candidates.** A related alert that fully resolved before the incident window began needs explicit justification (a concrete mechanism with in-window evidence) before you may implicate it. No overlap and no mechanism → not the cause.
4.  **Correlated/assembly alerts are CANDIDATES, not conclusions.** When triage correlation or incident assembly lists related alerts, each entry is unverified. Mark one "Confirmed" only when you cite an observation showing window overlap or precedence AND a causal path to the primary resource. Otherwise mark it "Not assessed". Never build the causal chain around a candidate you have not verified.
5.  **Do not blame the observer.** The monitoring and analysis system leaves footprints in the environment it observes: its agents and collectors run inside the monitored clusters and cloud accounts, and this investigation's own activity shows up too — its commands in audit trails and access logs, its queries in live database activity views, and (when the analysis runtime is self-hosted in a monitored cluster) its LLM/agent errors in application logs. Evidence that describes the monitoring/analysis tooling itself, or that was generated while you were investigating, is an observer effect until proven otherwise: before using it, verify its timestamp falls inside the incident window and that it lies on the primary resource's request path.
6.  **The cause must explain the full failure surface.** Enumerate the distinct failing paths, endpoints, or components visible in the evidence and test every candidate cause against all of them. A valid root cause accounts for everything that failed — if the failures span paths the candidate does not serve, the cause lies in something all of those paths share, not in a dependency of just one of them. If your candidate leaves any observed failure unexplained, say so and keep investigating instead of concluding.
7.  **Follow the chain to a mechanism — naming a suspect service is not a conclusion.** When your evidence places the cause inside another service, that service becomes your investigation subject: pull ITS logs, metrics, traces, and deploy history for the same incident window, and recurse until you reach a concrete mechanism (a saturated pool, a lock, a bad config value, a failing dependency) or you have genuinely exhausted what your tools can retrieve. For a service that is slow or unresponsive across endpoints, generic container CPU/memory is NOT a sufficient saturation check: services export their own pool/queue/client metrics. Enumerate the metric families actually recorded for the suspect service (metrics discovery / series-match tools) and inspect connection-pool, in-use, wait, and queue-depth series over the incident window before concluding.
8.  **Past windows live in the data stores, not the pods.** The incident window may be hours or days in the past. `kubectl logs` only covers the current pod lifetime — an empty result after a restart proves nothing and must not end the trail. For past windows, query the log store (logs tools with absolute times) and the metrics store (range queries over the window); they retain history far longer than pods do.
9.  **Read failure semantics correctly, and demand a discriminating signal.** On an outbound call, `context canceled` means the CALLING request was aborted (its client gave up or its own deadline fired) — it is evidence the caller was stalled, not that the call's target failed; `context deadline exceeded` or connection errors are what implicate the target. And a root-cause candidate must explain the incident's timing: a signal that was also present at a similar level for hours before onset (a chronic/background alert) cannot explain a sharply bounded incident — find what CHANGED at onset (a saturation metric crossing its limit, a deploy, a restart) and anchor the cause there.
